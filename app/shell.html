How movement works
  • Each region's angle is spread over its motion segments by measured in vivo shares; no single joint takes a region's range.
  • Controls reach 85 % of mean active ranges. Combined motions share one envelope per region, so all axes cannot peak together; every segment is capped.
  • Ribs move with their vertebrae and may turn up to 10° at their costovertebral joints; the sternum settles where the cartilages are disturbed least; cartilages bend and keep their length.
  • Clavicles hang from the manubrium (sternoclavicular joint), never from the spine. Each scapula hangs from its clavicle at the acromioclavicular joint and glides over the rib cage at a soft-tissue distance; it has no bony joint with the ribs or spine.
  • Shoulder controls: raise / lower and forward / back move the clavicle (85 % of sternoclavicular ranges); upward rotation turns the scapula (85 % of the isolated, arm-free range). The clavicle's axial rotation and the scapula's tilt and internal rotation are solved so the scapula stays on the rib cage. One envelope per side.
  • The pelvis is the root: tilt, hip hike and turn move both hip bones and the sacrum together about the hip axis, once; the spine's own angles ride on top.
  • Pelvic joints move only a little and separately from the whole pelvis: the sacrum may nod ±2° against both hip bones (sacroiliac nutation) and the hip bones may counter-rotate ±0.5° each, shearing the symphysis by up to ≈ 1.5 mm.
  • The hyoid has no bony joint; until the head and neck soft tissue exist it follows C3.
  • Attachment frames wait at both acetabula (femurs), both glenoids (humeri) and the atlas (skull); none of those bones is built.
